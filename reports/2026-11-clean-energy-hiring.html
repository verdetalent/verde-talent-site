Clean Energy Hiring Report · November 2026

Going from solar tech to lineworker doubles your pay. Lineworker jobs almost never ask for a degree.

Field pay in clean energy climbs like a ladder, and the rungs are electrical skills, from $57,595 for a solar technician to $117,146 for a lineworker. Plus: batteries are a third of what America is building but less than a quarter of the jobs, wind led this month’s new openings, and solar O&M technician is the role to watch.

By Adam Heugel, founder of Verde Talent · Published November 9, 2026 · Data as of November 9, 2026

From Adam, founder of Verde Talent

A note to employers: if you’re struggling to fill battery and line roles, look one rung down. The solar techs and electricians in this report already have most of the skills, and a few months of training can close the gap. Hiring from inside the field is faster than waiting for the perfect résumé.

The ladder from solar tech to lineworker

A solar O&M technician starts around $57,595. A lineworker earns $117,146, about twice as much, and only 4% of lineworker postings mention a degree. Each step up is an electrical skill employers name in their postings, and each role links to its full salary benchmark.

What moves you up a rung

  • Battery work (+$8,965). Battery storage technicians post a median of $66,560. About half their postings mention relays (52%) or SCADA control systems (48%).
  • A journeyman card. Electricians ($76,981) and lineworkers both come up through apprenticeships: 63% of electrician and 65% of lineworker postings ask for journeyman level, and only 6% of electrician postings mention a degree.
  • Substation and relay work ($92,220). The one rung where a degree usually comes up, more often a two-year associate degree than a bachelor’s.
  • A CDL for line work. 44% of lineworker postings ask for a commercial driver’s license, to drive the bucket trucks.

Batteries are a third of what America is building, and less than a quarter of the jobs

Battery storage makes up 31% of the solar, wind and storage capacity scheduled to be built (67 GW) but only 23% of open jobs across those three sectors, and 37 GW of it is scheduled to come online by the end of 2027. Wind is the reverse: 11% of what’s being built and 24% of the jobs. Wind turbine technician is the most common wind opening, and much of that work is keeping wind farms that are already running in service.

For job seekers, battery skills are the first rung on the ladder above, worth about $8,965 more than solar O&M work.

Wind led this month’s new openings

Comparing the same employers on October 5 and today, open wind roles went from 406 to 476, a 17% rise and more new openings than solar, storage and grid added between them. Most of the growth is at Vestas, the turbine maker, which added 66 open roles, mostly wind turbine technicians. 103 of its 180 US postings offer a sign-on bonus, usually $2,500 for technicians who already hold GWO safety training, so that certificate is worth getting before you apply.

The internship window is closing

Internship and co-op openings at the same employers fell from 208 to 90. Most of the drop is Xcel Energy, which had 127 internships open in October and has 8 now. Other entry-level roles held steady. If you’re a student, apply to the internships still open now rather than waiting for the new year.

Role of the month

Solar O&M technician

The technicians who keep solar farms — and, more and more, the batteries beside them — running: inspecting panels and inverters, fixing faults and answering outages. Employers are hiring for it in 33 states, and openings at the same employers rose from 178 to 198 since October.

Where the openings are
Open solar O&M technician roles, top six states.

Texas and California lead with 38 and 33 openings. NextEra Energy posts the most (34), followed by SOLV Energy (25) and Invenergy (24) — a mix of project owners and the contractors that run sites for them.

What employers ask for

This is one of the more open doors in clean energy: 71% of postings mention a high school diploma or GED, and a degree is rarely required. What employers do expect is a driver’s license (68%) and a willingness to travel between sites (60%). 60% mention battery storage, so electrical and battery experience goes a long way. NABCEP, solar’s best-known certification, is mentioned in only 2%.

How this report is built. Figures are open US job postings on Verde Talent on November 9, 2026, across the 104 energy companies we track. Month-on-month comparisons use only the 43 employers that had US roles open on both October 5 (the date of the October report) and today; 26 employers added to Verde Talent since are left out of them, so a change is a change in hiring, not in coverage. Pay is the median of what postings advertise, not offers made or salaries paid. “Mentions” are postings whose title or description names the item. Planned capacity is the EIA generator inventory, solar, wind and storage only; shares of open jobs count postings tagged solar, wind or storage. The workforce figure is the U.S. Energy & Employment Report (2026 edition) for solar, wind, storage and smart grid. The open-jobs total includes a small number of roles outside the US; everything else in this report is US only.
